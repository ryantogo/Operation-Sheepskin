# Operation Sheepskin Gone Hot

**Anguilla, March 19, 1969 (hypothetical scenario)**
Companion narrative for `operation-sheepskin-gone-hot.json` (170-second playback)

## Premise

On March 19, 1969, British paratroopers and police landed on Anguilla to end the island's unilateral secession. The real Operation Sheepskin met no resistance; Anguillians greeted the troops on the beach, and the "invasion" became a byword for imperial farce.

This scenario diverges at a single point. Although the historical Anguillian Defence Force buried its rifles before the landing, here it digs in instead, because a small detachment of Cuban "volunteers" has arrived in the weeks after the Whitlock fiasco. Havana supplies what the islanders lacked: Soviet-pattern DShK heavy machine guns, an 82mm mortar, improvised technicals, and a cadre of men who have seen fire before. The British plan stays exactly as written; only the reception changes.

## Order of Battle

**United Kingdom**
- HMS Minerva (Leander-class frigate) and HMS Rothesay, providing 4.5-inch naval gunfire
- Two Westland Wasp helicopters
- A rifle company of 2nd Battalion, The Parachute Regiment, organized as two landing-craft platoons, one airmobile platoon, and a company reserve
- A Metropolitan Police detachment, landing once the ground is secure

**Anguilla / Cuban Volunteers**
- Anguillian Defence Force militia at Sandy Ground and The Quarter
- Cuban volunteer infantry stiffening both positions
- Two DShK heavy machine guns, one on the ridge above Sandy Ground and one covering Wallblake airstrip
- An 82mm mortar team on the reverse slope inland of the beach
- Two technicals blocking the Wallblake and Valley roads

## Narrative

### Before dawn (0:00 to 0:30)
The task force rides at anchor in Road Bay, its frigates silhouetted against a paling sky. Landing craft carrying 1 and 2 Platoons form up off Sandy Ground while the Wasps lift 3 Platoon east toward Wallblake. The plan assumes a walkover; nobody aboard expects a shot.

### The ambush (0:26 to 0:50)
The first sign of trouble comes from the airstrip. As the second Wasp approaches Wallblake, the ridge-mounted DShK opens up, raking the airframe and forcing it to abort. Moments later the mortar on the reverse slope begins dropping rounds onto the Sandy Ground shingle, and when the lead landing craft grounds, the beach DShK catches 1 Platoon in the open. The Paras take their first casualties within seconds of stepping ashore.

### Naval gunfire (0:50 to 1:32)
Pinned on the beach, 1 Platoon's radio operator calls for naval support. HMS Minerva answers first, walking 4.5-inch shells across the ridge until the beach DShK falls silent. HMS Rothesay then turns her guns inland, hunting the mortar position; its destruction lifts the pressure on the beachhead, and the Paras begin pushing off the sand toward the interior.

### Wallblake (1:34 to 1:56)
At the airstrip, 3 Platoon fights without naval cover. Its sections clear the roadblock technical first, then work methodically uphill against the second DShK. The position falls only after close assault, but its capture gives the British a secure landing ground and removes the last threat to their helicopters.

### The Quarter (1:44 to 2:28)
The hardest fighting comes at the crossroads, where the Cuban cadre and the remaining militia have prepared their strongest position. Small-arms fire stalls 2 Platoon's advance and wounds several men. Since the company reserve has now landed and is moving up behind them, the British can afford to wait for another fire mission: Minerva destroys the technical on the Valley road, and the reserve's arrival on the flank convinces the defenders that the position cannot hold. The Cubans and militia withdraw rather than die in place.

### The Valley (2:08 to 2:50)
With the crossroads abandoned, the Metropolitan Police detachment finally comes ashore at Sandy Ground, roughly where the planners had imagined it arriving hours earlier. The reserve pushes on into The Valley, the island's capital, and by mid-afternoon 2 PARA holds every objective.

## Outcome

Britain wins, as it was always going to win; a rifle company backed by two frigates outclasses a militia on an island sixteen miles long. The victory, however, costs something the real operation never did: British casualties, Anguillian and Cuban dead, and a Cold War incident in a Caribbean colony. Where history remembers Sheepskin as a punchline, this version would have handed Moscow and Havana a propaganda gift and handed Whitehall a debate about why it had sent paratroopers to fight for an island that had only wanted to be left alone.
